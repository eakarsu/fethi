This video is an economics analysis that discusses how, in the digital age, **ownership** is “quietly dying” and how this creates a new kind of economic dependence or new slavery.

## Basic info about the video
- Channel: “Kayıt Dışı İktisat” – Ceyhun Elgin  
- Main theme: How ownership is weakening, and how subscription economy and planned obsolescence erode the middle class’s ability to accumulate wealth.  

## Main concepts and mechanisms
The video explains three mechanisms through which ownership is dissolving: shrinkflation, the subscription economy, and planned obsolescence.

1. Shrinkflation and skimpflation  
   - Shrinkflation: The price stays the same while the amount of product decreases (for example, a 400 g package becomes 350 g).  
   - Skimpflation: The price stays the same while the quality of the product or service falls (less meat, weaker ingredients, fewer services, less cleaning, etc.).  
   - These changes don’t fully show up in official inflation numbers and invisibly reduce the purchasing power of especially low‑income households.  

2. Subscription economy  
   - The shift from “owning” to “accessing” is described: Spotify, Netflix, e‑books, cloud storage, software, and similar services disappear the moment you stop paying.  
   - Small monthly payments (“drip pricing”) add up to large amounts over time, and people systematically underestimate this total cost.  
   - “Zombie subscribers”: People who hardly use the service but do not cancel; they are an important source of profit for companies.  
   - This model prevents wealth accumulation and turns life into a system where you continuously pay but do not build up assets you truly own.  

3. Planned obsolescence and digital rights  
   - The historical roots are linked to the Great Depression of the 1930s, when the idea of designing products with a built‑in “death date” appeared.  
   - Examples: Non‑replaceable batteries, phones slowed down with updates, repair being blocked, and the use of DRM so that usage is controlled by license terms.  
   - Legally the device is yours, but in practice the company keeps control, which empties out the real meaning of property rights.  

## Theoretical and ideological frame
The speaker refers to several thinkers to frame what is happening.

- Marx: Argues that in the 21st century expropriation happens not by force but “voluntarily,” wrapped in language of flexibility and convenience.  
- Hayek: Emphasizes that property rights are a basic guarantee of individual freedom.  
- Veblen: Says we moved from conspicuous consumption to a kind of “conspicuous‑less dependence,” where people spend but do not even gain status.  
- Polanyi: When markets transform property relations too far, society develops defensive reactions; “right to repair” movements are given as an example.  

The core claim: From both a free‑market and a structural‑critical perspective, the emerging model ends up weakening the individual’s position.

## Policy examples and possible solutions
The video offers both individual and structural ideas for solutions.

Individual level:  
- Check unit prices.  
- Track your subscriptions and cancel the ones you don’t really use.  
- Support and use repair instead of replacement where possible, and defend the right to repair.  

Regulation and policy level:  
- France’s rule that forces companies to inform consumers when product weight/size is reduced.  
- South Korea’s fines on producers that change product size without clearly informing consumers.  
- The EU’s right‑to‑repair directive and California’s right‑to‑repair law (SB244).  
- Legislative attempts in the US to limit shrinkflation and efforts by regulators to tackle hidden fees.  

Alternative models:  
- Cooperatives, open‑source software, community‑supported agriculture, “repair cafés,” and similar initiatives are presented as practices that bring the idea of ownership back to communities.  

## Where the video lands
At the end, the speaker invites viewers to question their own lives: In the last 10 years, have the assets you truly own increased, or only the number of services you can access and the list of monthly payments you make?  
The answer to this, he argues, will largely shape the class structure of the future and the divide between “those who own” and “those who rent.”
